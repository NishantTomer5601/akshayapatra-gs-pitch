# AIF Semifinal — 8-Minute Verbal Pitch · DRAFT V2
**Built for an international partner audience. No mythology, no founding history, no India-specific references that require explanation.**

**Team:** Nishant Tomer · Pratham Goenka · Aadithya Muralidharan · Somya Sethi · Mohajit Neog
**Organisation:** The Akshaya Patra Foundation · **Grant:** $250,000

**~1,090 words ≈ 8:00 at 135 wpm.** Five sections of ~215 words ≈ 1:30 each.
Notes in *[brackets]* are **not spoken**.

**What changed from V1:** the Mahabharata metaphor, the year-2000 origin story, and the growth arc are gone. The pitch now opens on the operation as it runs tomorrow morning, and closes on the same morning — a bookend that needs no cultural context. Every remaining reference is legible anywhere in the world.

---

## SPEAKER 1 — WHAT IT IS *(1:30)*
### Job: from zero to full comprehension in ninety seconds. No history — present tense only.

Tomorrow morning at four o'clock, in eighty industrial kitchens across India, about five thousand people will start cooking.

By eleven, **two point three five million children** will have eaten lunch.

Not a symbolic lunch. Hot food, cooked that morning, delivered into **twenty-five thousand government schools**, and eaten before it drops below sixty-five degrees.

The organisation that does this is called **Akshaya Patra.** Most people outside India have never heard of it. It is the largest school meal programme operated by any non-profit anywhere in the world — **five billion meals** served to date.

The model is a public-private partnership, and it's worth understanding, because it's why the economics work. The government assigns the schools and funds roughly half the cost of every meal. Akshaya Patra builds the kitchens, buys the food, cooks it, and delivers it. Donors cover what's left — which is about **seven cents a meal.**

And the purpose is narrower than you'd expect. This is not a nutrition programme. It is an **attendance** programme. A hungry child stops coming to school. The meal is the thing that keeps her in the room.

We spent three months on this organisation. It started by getting on a plane to Bengaluru.

> **HANDOFF →** Speaker 2 starts on the kitchen floor. No greeting, no name, no "thank you". Walk straight in.

---

## SPEAKER 2 — HOW IT WORKS *(1:30)*
### Job: earn operational respect. They should stop hearing "charity" and start hearing "manufacturing".

**The kitchen we visited starts at four in the morning.**

By the time we got there, rice was already cooking — a thousand children's worth, every fifteen minutes. Beside it, a machine producing **forty thousand flatbreads an hour.** The building is designed around gravity: ingredients go in at the top, cooked food comes out at the bottom, and no human hand touches it in between.

One roof. A hundred thousand children.

*[Beat. Change tone — this is where it stops being a tour and becomes a system.]*

And then the constraint that reframed this for us.

The food doesn't leave in a truck. It leaves **against a clock.** Cooked rice has to be sealed into a steel vessel and be in front of a child while it is still above **sixty-five degrees.** Fourteen hundred vehicles. Seventy-kilometre routes. A **fifteen-minute delivery window** at every school.

There is no warehouse in this supply chain. There is no inventory. Food is manufactured and consumed on the same morning, or it is waste.

And there is **no buffer day.** If tomorrow's plan is wrong, you don't correct it next week. A child eats less tomorrow.

We went in expecting a charity that needed money.

**We found a manufacturing operation running at genuine world-class discipline — with two points where it is running completely blind.**

> **HANDOFF →** Speaker 3 picks up "blind" in their opening sentence.

---

## SPEAKER 3 — THE TWO BLIND SPOTS *(1:30)*
### **The most important ninety seconds in the pitch.** Owns the reveal.

**The first blind spot is at the front of the chain.**

Every night, someone decides what eighty kitchens will buy tomorrow. And it's decided the way it has been decided for twenty-five years — on experience, on a calendar, on a considered guess. Attendance moves. Weather moves. Prices move. The guess doesn't.

Here's what that costs. In January, Akshaya Patra threw away **fifty-four thousand kilograms of vegetables.** Not spoilage from carelessness — over-buying, because nobody could know. That is more than **one full day of lunch for all two point three five million children.** Bought, paid for, transported — and never a meal.

**The second blind spot is at the end of the chain.** Fourteen hundred vehicles on the road every morning, and not one of them can be seen. Do you know how Akshaya Patra learns that lunch arrived? *The school calls them.* A teacher, with a phone.

*[Full stop. Slow down. This next passage is the pivot of the entire pitch.]*

But here is what we did not expect to find.

We pulled the capacity file for all eighty kitchens. **Akshaya Patra is not running out of kitchen.** There is room, today, for another **three hundred thousand meals a day.** The buildings are up. The equipment is installed. The staff are on payroll. One of their largest kitchens is running at **thirty-six percent** of what it can produce.

**They are not short of capacity. They are short of certainty.**

So we asked a simple question: what does certainty cost?

> **HANDOFF →** Speaker 4's first words answer that question directly. Don't restate it.

---

## SPEAKER 4 — THE BUILD *(1:30)*
### Job: make the ask feel obvious — and make clear this is *their* plan, funded, not our idea, imposed.

**Two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.**

And we didn't invent how to spend it. They had already worked it out.

We put the question to their leadership directly: if Goldman Sachs handed you this money tomorrow, where does it do the most good? Every instinct says they answer *"build us another kitchen."*

**They ranked that second.**

What they ranked first was the technology.

So — **a hundred and fifty thousand dollars** against the first gap. One forecasting engine across all eighty kitchens, reading attendance, school calendars, menus and weather, and converting them into tomorrow's order. Buying each commodity when and where it is cheapest, rather than whenever it runs out. Waste from five percent, down toward one.

**A hundred thousand dollars** against the second. GPS and door sensors across all fourteen hundred vehicles, geofenced to every school, so that a delivery confirms *itself* before the bell rings.

And on that second one, we are not designing anything. There is a twelve-page specification sitting on a server in Bengaluru — servers, sensors, database, integration points. It was written this July by an intern on their own IT team.

They have known precisely what to build for a year.

**They ran out of money before they ran out of ideas.**

So what does two hundred and fifty thousand dollars actually buy?

> **HANDOFF →** Speaker 5 answers with a number, immediately. No preamble, no restatement.

---

## SPEAKER 5 — THE RETURN *(1:30)*
### Job: make the arithmetic feel inevitable, then land quietly and stop.

**Fifteen dollars and fifty-three cents.** That is what it costs to feed one child for an entire school year — two hundred and thirty-two days.

So here is the whole case, in two numbers.

Spend two hundred and fifty thousand dollars on **food**, and you feed **sixteen thousand children** for one year. Then it's gone.

Spend it on the **system**, and five percent comes off a forty-six-million-dollar annual food bill. That saving alone feeds **a hundred and fifty thousand children.** And there is already room for them — in kitchens nobody has to build.

Then it happens again the following year. And the year after that.

**The cheque is written once. The saving recurs for as long as the organisation exists.**

*[Beat. Lower the volume for the final passage. Do not rush it.]*

We looked hard for the version of this where Goldman Sachs writes a cheque and children eat. It exists. It is worth sixteen thousand children, once.

This is the other version.

Nothing about tomorrow morning changes. The kitchens still open at four. The vehicles still leave at seven. The same people cook the same food in the same buildings.

The only difference is that a hundred and fifty thousand more children are sitting at the other end of it.

Same operation. Same morning. **Two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.**

Thank you.

# WHY IT'S BUILT THIS WAY

**The bookend replaces the metaphor.** Speaker 1 opens on *"tomorrow morning at four o'clock."* Speaker 5 closes on *"nothing about tomorrow morning changes."* It requires zero cultural knowledge, and it makes the grant feel like an adjustment to something already in motion rather than the launch of something new — which is precisely the argument.

**The three engineered moments:**
1. **Open** — *"By eleven, two point three five million children will have eaten lunch."* Scale delivered as a fact about tomorrow, not as a statistic about the past.
2. **Drop** — *"They are not short of capacity. They are short of certainty."* Speaker 3, ~4:15 in. Reframes the problem from money to information and pays off everything preceding it.
3. **Close** — *"Same operation. Same morning."* No crescendo, no appeal. The restraint is what makes it land with this audience.

**Twelve numbers, spoken once each.** None is announced; each arrives as the answer to something the audience is already wondering.

**Deliberately not said:** 655 vendors · 78 buyers / 145 approvers · 700 line items · 200,000 vessels · $10.8M cumulative · the 2026–29 projection · dropout rates · Global Hunger Index · ISO certifications · ERP product names. Q&A only. Speaking any of them costs more time than it buys credibility.

**The handoff rule:** every speaker ends on an unresolved tension; the next resolves it in their first sentence. Nobody summarises, nobody re-introduces the organisation, nobody says "and now my colleague will cover…". It should sound like one argument in five voices.

**Casting:** Speaker 3 carries the pitch — give it to whoever can slow down and hold a silence. Speaker 5 needs the steadiest voice; the final six lines fail if rushed. Speaker 1 must sound unhurried despite carrying the most information.

**If you run long:** cut *"There is no warehouse… or it is waste"* from Speaker 2 (~10s) and *"We looked hard for the version… once"* from Speaker 5 (~12s). Cut nothing from Speaker 3.
